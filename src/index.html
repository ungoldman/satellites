      Satellites

      Earth observation satellites with public imagery, mapped in real time based on available data. Winged icons are geostationary and stay over one spot on the equator, with a view of a whole side of the Earth. Arrow icons are polar and image a strip at a time as they pass. Select one to see the ground it covers and its latest picture.

      Orbits

      • Geostationary. 35,786 km up, one lap a day the same way the Earth turns, so each stays over one point. The view turns with the Earth too, so they hold still while the polar ones sweep past.
      • Sun-synchronous. 700 to 830 km up, about 100 minutes a lap over the poles, crossing the equator at the same local time every day.
      • Scale. By default height is squeezed on a log so both kinds show. At true scale geostationary is 6.6 Earth radii out.

      How it works

      • Orbits come from CelesTrak's mean elements, fetched for the page every two hours, and are solved with Kepler's equation plus the nodal precession from the Earth's bulge. That stays within about 50 km of SGP4 over three days, checked against satellite.js. If the newest elements are older than that, the clock stops at their time and shows where the satellites were then.
      • The globe is ray-cast per pixel in WebGL. Each pixel finds its latitude and longitude, reads the height or depth there, and is lit by the Sun, which draws the terminator. Coast, contours, and grid come from the same heights.
      • A full disk's caption gives the time of the image. A daily composite has no one time, so its caption gives the satellite's daylight pass over the area shown, worked out from its orbit.
      • The view stays north up. Dragging keeps the ground under the pointer, and the keys move the center over the ground at a steady rate.
